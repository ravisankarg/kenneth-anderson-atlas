THE PERSON BEHIND THE STORIES

A life between city and forest

Born in Bolarum near Secunderabad in 1910, Kenneth Douglas Stewart Anderson grew up in Bangalore. He wrote about encounters with wildlife and forest communities across southern India, while also hunting big game. His accounts helped inspire later interest in conservation, though they remain the perspective of a hunter writing in a colonial-era tradition.

His first collection, Nine Man-Eaters and One Rogue, appeared in 1954. Its stories are the starting point for this atlas—not a complete record of every journey.

A LANDSCAPE WITHOUT HARD EDGES

Bengaluru at the edge of a living network

South of the city, Bannerghatta’s wooded hills link with the Hosur and Cauvery landscapes. Farther southwest, the wider Eastern and Western Ghats meet through the hills around Sathyamangalam, the Nilgiris and Bandipur. Animals use habitat across administrative boundaries.

In Anderson’s time, these were not empty wilderness: villages, fields, grazing lands, forests and open grasslands coexisted. Land use has since changed; connections persist but are pressured by roads, settlements and other barriers.

Read this map as a concept, not a 1950 survey. The glowing bands illustrate broad relationships described in modern ecological sources. Why movement matters

Elephants need large ranges for food, water and seasonal movement. Other species use landscapes differently: a connection suitable for an elephant is not automatically a route for every animal.

What can interrupt it?

Roads, fences, quarrying, settlement and cultivation can constrict movement. Conservation also requires attention to people who live and farm in these shared landscapes.

Evidence, not a time machine

Modern corridor records document present-day relationships. They offer context for Anderson’s era, but cannot establish the exact extent of the 1950 forest or reconstruct individual journeys.
